# Crazy at Work: A Reflection and Review
"It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" is a rejection of the chaos worshipped by hustle culture in the modern workplace. 
Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH), founders of Basecamp, argue that a calm, focused work environment leads to better productivity and employee satisfaction. 
They don’t just question hustle culture—they call out its evils. 
Meetings? Mostly useless. 
Deadlines? Rarely real. 
MS Teams? A digital anxiety machine. 
They walk through the practices Basecamp lives by: what’s stuck, what’s been scrapped, and how they’ve been able to build a "calm" company.


In this post, I'm pulling out some of my favorite insights as well as some of my own reflections on the book. 
The structure of this post will mimic that of the book, divided into seven parts, each discussing (and debating) key principles from the book.
* First
* Curb Your Ambition
* Defend Your Time
* Feed Your Culture

## Curb Your Ambition
Basecamp's small company culture (as DHH and Fried describe it in this book) is an interesting contrast from big companies.
There's a stereotype around the words "small" and "big" as they relate to companies.
Small tends to mean startup, fast, and risky whereas big tends to mean established, slow, and stable. 
Basecamp doesn't seem to follow those stereotypes as in this section DHH and Fried discuss their 

The authors argue that ambition in the workplace is often poorly directed and misplaced.
They don't say that goals can be a double edged sword, they discuss how Basecamp doesn't use any goals.
This has an obvious level of controversy because the initial thought is: "what do you mean your company doesn't have goals?"


Their company runs on a unique schedule, instead of using the traditional fiscal year or quarter, they use 6 week development cycles with breaks of 2 weeks to reflect, reset, and prepare for the next 6 week cycle.

An area they don't discuss in detail, but I think is relevant here is the word "impact".
Many of the reasons that big companies operate they do is because of this word.
I think this concept significantly inhibits the ability of companies to move forward and truly make an impact.
At a big company, where dozens of people have the same exact role, operating in different spaces, it feels important to need to make an impact and differentiate.

## Defend Your Time
This section of the book is truly phenomenal.
Our time is incredibly precious and many companies don't act like it.
Software like MS Teams and Slack makes it so easy for others to identify the perfect time for them to interrupt your day with their needed help.
The authors say "A meeting costs absolutely nothing to the person making it, but it cost you everything." *VALIDATE THIS*
When calendars are structured around finding time for everyone, workdays turn into ADHD simulator.
It takes time to focus, and having a day with periods of 30 minute sessions on/off between meetings severely harms the efficiency and happiness of people.

## Feed Your Culture
DHH and Fried have been around as the Basecamp founders for a minute.
They've made mistakes and tried a lot of company culture practices to see what works.
My favorite practice that I haven't seen at pretty much any other company is the idea of paid vacation, not PTO.

Basecamp doesn't do bonuses or PTO, instead employees have 30 days that they can use for vacation and 7 days that they can use for personal time *VALIDATE THIS*. 
The difference here is that employee vacations are fully paid for by Basecamp.
Your flight, hotel, meals, all paid for for the entire family (up to $5K per vacation).
I am in love with this.
The way I handle my money, a bonus means absolutely nothing to me if not just a variable component to my salary.
